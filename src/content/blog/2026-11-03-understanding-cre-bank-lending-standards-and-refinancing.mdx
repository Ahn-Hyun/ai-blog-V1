# How shifting bank lending standards and regulatory guidance shape CRE refinancing

How do shifts in bank lending standards and regulatory guidelines impact commercial real estate refinancing and loan restructurings?

Maturing commercial real estate (CRE) loans face refinancing friction primarily driven by bank credit availability. Meanwhile, recent regulatory updates have narrowed supervisory oversight to focus on quantifiable balance-sheet metrics. As depository institutions adjust their lending posture, the ability of borrowers to refinance cleanly or restructure troubled debt increasingly hinges on fundamental credit indicators, such as debt-service coverage and collateral values, rather than subjective bank considerations.

### Tracking credit availability: the SLOOS benchmark

The Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS) is a primary benchmark for tracking credit availability in the United States. As summarized by [Trepp](https://www.trepp.com/sloos-cre-lending), the SLOOS is a quarterly survey that monitors changes in bank lending standards and loan demand across major credit categories.

Tracking these quarterly shifts gives market participants a directional indicator of banking sector liquidity, signaling whether institutions are adopting a restrictive or accommodative stance toward prospective borrowers. When survey results show tightening standards or softening demand, maturing properties face higher hurdles to secure replacement financing.

Available evidence remains limited to the survey’s quarterly cadence and its broad monitoring of standards and demand. Detailed empirical findings regarding specific covenant adjustments, property-level commercial real estate categories, or divergence by institution size are not documented within the current dataset and remain subject to the specific disclosures of each quarterly release.

### Regulatory taxonomy: OCC removes reputation risk

Supervisory frameworks govern the parameters national banks must follow when evaluating real estate credit risk. Effective March 20, 2025, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) removed all references to reputation risk from its *Commercial Real Estate Lending* booklet of the *Comptroller’s Handbook*, identifying the deletions through strikethroughs pursuant to OCC Bulletin 2025-4, as documented in the updated [Commercial Real Estate Lending 2.0](https://www.occ.gov/publications-and-resources/publications/comptrollers-handbook/files/commercial-real-estate-lending/pub-ch-commercial-real-estate.pdf) guidance.

This revision formally removes reputation risk from the supervisory risk taxonomy used by OCC examiners to evaluate CRE lending programs. In loan workouts and restructurings, examiner assessments will no longer encompass standalone reputation risk considerations when evaluating how institutions handle troubled borrowers or renegotiate loan terms. Scrutiny instead remains anchored to the core safety and soundness risk categories retained in the handbook:
* Credit risk
* Compliance risk
* Liquidity risk
* Operational risk

This shift narrows the formal criteria examiners can cite when reviewing loan modifications. Supervisory oversight focuses directly on quantifiable credit administration metrics, such as borrower repayment capacity, debt-service coverage ratios, and collateral valuations. However, because the evidence confirms only the procedural removal of reputation risk references rather than an overhaul of workout guidelines, it remains uncertain whether this revision meaningfully changes day-to-day examiner behavior during restructurings or simply streamlines supervisory taxonomy by eliminating subjective reputation criteria.

When commercial real estate loans approach maturity, a borrower's ability to refinance cleanly or avoid distressed disposition depends heavily on prevailing bank lending standards and regulatory guidelines ([Trepp](https://www.trepp.com/sloos-cre-lending); [Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)](https://www.occ.gov/publications-and-resources/publications/comptrollers-handbook/files/commercial-real-estate-lending/pub-ch-commercial-real-estate.pdf)).

Supervisory frameworks incorporate metrics such as loan-to-value (LTV) limits and debt yield to evaluate collateral coverage and income-generating capacity. Even so, the available evidence does not provide quantitative thresholds or property-level data defining the exact scale of loans that will refinance cleanly versus those forced into liquidation. Because broader macroeconomic shifts and bank-specific constraints interact at the asset level, the precise rate at which tighter underwriting standards will trigger forced dispositions across the maturity wave remains uncertain without further asset-level and portfolio-specific empirical evidence.

For market participants managing upcoming debt maturities, the regulatory narrowing at the OCC clarifies the supervisory baseline: reviews during workouts are explicitly tied to safety and soundness metrics rather than reputational exposure. However, regulatory clarification does not ease credit on its own. If quarterly SLOOS data points to restrictive bank lending standards, property owners must still meet strict, objective credit metrics, leaving little margin for underperforming properties facing refinancing deadlines.
