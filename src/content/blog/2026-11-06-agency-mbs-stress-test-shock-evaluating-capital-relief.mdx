# The agency MBS stress test shock: capital relief or regulatory illusion?

The Federal Reserve softened the hypothetical shock applied to agency pass-through securities in its 2026 stress testing scenarios, but large banks will not receive immediate capital relief. Because the Federal Reserve concurrently froze Stress Capital Buffer (SCB) requirements for 2026 while finalizing structural revisions to the framework, binding capital requirements for participating banks remain unchanged. As a result, theoretical market transmission from reduced dealer balance-sheet costs to narrower mortgage spreads and cheaper home loans remains unproven.

## The GMS recalibration: milder assumptions on paper

For the 2026 supervisory stress test cycle, the Federal Reserve set December 31, 2025, as the official balance sheet and income statement cut-off date for participating institutions, as detailed in the [Federal Reserve 2026 Stress Test Scenarios](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-stress-test-scenarios.htm). This date anchors the baseline portfolios used to model hypothetical revenues, losses, and capital levels across a nine-quarter supervisory horizon.

In finalizing the 2026 scenarios, the Board adjusted the Global Market Shock (GMS) component by reducing the shock magnitudes applied to agency pass-through securities and certain commodities relative to its initial proposals, according to the [Federal Reserve 2026 Stress Test Scenarios](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-stress-test-scenarios.htm).

Because the GMS models an instantaneous mark-to-market revaluation of trading portfolios and private equity holdings, severe stress on agency MBS generates paper losses that erode projected post-stress common equity tier 1 (CET1) capital. By dampening the severity of hypothetical spread widening and price drops on agency MBS, the Federal Reserve softened the simulated capital depletion associated with holding agency debt. In theory, this lower regulatory penalty reduces the marginal balance-sheet cost for dealer banks to intermediate and warehouse agency pass-through positions compared to the harsher proposed calibration.

The Federal Reserve's public summary did not publish the exact basis-point adjustments applied to agency yields, nor did it disclose firm-by-firm reductions in hypothetical capital charges. The precise balance-sheet capacity freed by the scenario adjustment cannot be quantified from public supervisory data alone.

## The policy disconnect: frozen capital buffers

The relief the scenario adjustment offered on paper did not translate into lower regulatory capital stacks in practice.

On June 24, 2026, the Federal Reserve published stress test results for the 32 large banks participating in the cycle, projecting an aggregate peak-to-trough CET1 capital ratio decline of 1.6 percentage points over the nine-quarter stress horizon, as reported by the [Bank Policy Institute](https://bpi.com/the-2026-federal-reserve-stress-test-results-a-framework-in-transition).

In a standard cycle, an institution's maximum projected CET1 drawdown directly sets its binding Stress Capital Buffer. For 2026, that mechanical link was paused. The [Bank Policy Institute](https://bpi.com/the-2026-federal-reserve-stress-test-results-a-framework-in-transition) reported that SCB requirements will remain unchanged for 2026 while the Federal Reserve completes structural updates to its stress testing regime following a public notice-and-comment process.

Even though the 1.6 percentage point aggregate drawdown reported by the [Bank Policy Institute](https://bpi.com/the-2026-federal-reserve-stress-test-results-a-framework-in-transition) reflects the banking sector's loss-absorbing capacity under hypothetical stress, freezing the SCB prevents those stress projections from altering banks' binding capital buffer mandates for the year.

## Theory versus market reality in mortgage spreads

In standard financial theory, bank capital regulations directly influence fixed-income market liquidity. Bank-affiliated primary dealers intermediate agency MBS by holding inventory on their balance sheets. When regulatory constraints ease, dealers face lower holding costs, enabling them to compress the spread between agency mortgage yields and benchmark Treasuries, which could eventually reduce borrowing costs for retail mortgage borrowers.

Supervisory and market evidence indicates this transmission mechanism is currently interrupted:

1. Because SCB levels are frozen for 2026, large banks face the same binding regulatory capital hurdles as before, as documented by the [Bank Policy Institute](https://bpi.com/the-2026-federal-reserve-stress-test-results-a-framework-in-transition).
2. The supervisory record contains no empirical data tracking primary dealer MBS inventory absorption, mortgage-to-Treasury basis movements, or primary mortgage pricing.

Without a realized drop in binding capital requirements or empirical proof of dealer balance-sheet expansion, the idea that a milder GMS calibration is actively narrowing mortgage spreads or lowering homebuyer borrowing costs remains theoretical.

No. Retail mortgage rates depend on broader market factors, Treasury yields, and secondary market spreads. Because bank SCB requirements remain frozen for 2026 and supervisory records provide no direct data on dealer absorption or secondary spreads, the scenario change does not guarantee any reduction in consumer mortgage rates.

### Which financial institutions are directly subject to the Global Market Shock component?

The 2026 supervisory stress test evaluated 32 large banks, according to the [Bank Policy Institute](https://bpi.com/the-2026-federal-reserve-stress-test-results-a-framework-in-transition), with the GMS component specifically applied to large banking organizations that maintain substantial trading operations, dealer inventories, and private equity investments. Exact firm-by-firm parameter breakdowns and exposures were not detailed in the public scenario release.
